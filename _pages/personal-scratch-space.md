# Who am I?

I don't know! But I do know that I was born in Dhaka, Bangladesh. That's something I'm proud of; all Bengali people are taught to be proud of their culture, history, and language. I grew up primarily in a city called Saskatoon, SK: this is the city I probably love the most. It's in a pretty sparsely populated province, and still retains a lot of the "small-town vibe" I love, while continuing to have all the amenities you really need. I did my undergraduate studies in Toronto though. When I first moved to Toronto, the city scared me. I'd never seen buildings so big, I'd never gone on a subway, and I'd never lived alone. And indeed, I hated it for the first years I lived in the city. I was also the real studious type in my first few years of university. Never touched grass and all that. My friends slowly broke me out of that shell though, and by my final year of undergrad, the streets of Toronto felt like second nature to me. From the hustle of the early morning rush, to the quiet of a 2 AM stroll. I loved it. And now I'm in New York! Things will only get more fun from here I guess :). 

# What am I like?

I've heard... many opinions on what kind of person I'm like. Some people think I'm pretty feminine for a guy (I find this hilarious), some people think I'm intense, some think I'm caring, and some people think I'm funny. But the one I always get (up to different wording), is that I'm idiosyncratic. And that's probably the one I'd agree with the most. 

I did competitive debate up to the end of high school. I was awful for most of that time. It's a story I like to tell most people. Up to Grade 6, before provincials, I think I'd won something on the order of 2-3 rounds (give or take). After two years of debating! Think about how awful I must've been. I felt ashamed at times. Both on stage, where I'd stutter through incoherent sentences, and off stage, where I'd continue to feel like a failure. But I stuck with it, and by end of my high school, I'd accumulated my fair share of medals at the civic and provincial levels, and twice represented my province at national championships. I bring this up for two reasons. Because of my public speaking background, I am fundamentally someone who speaks his mind, for better or worse. And secondly, I'm not someone that gives up, again, for better or worse. If I start something, I see it through. One thing I convinced myself is that I would never be the type of person to leave a project, goal, or commitment hanging, especially if it involves others. And for the most part, I've stuck to this. And finally, I like telling this story, because it allows me to highlight that I'm not inherently smart or talented. Behind everything you see on my record, is years of painful work. 

I'm someone who likes to explore. I love trying new things, exploring new places, eating new food (this one's important), and starting new projects. I like and need freedom. This is especially present in my work; take a look at my publications in my CV and you'll see what I mean. Sometimes this isn't a good thing. One thing I've steadily been realising, is that throughout undergrad, I had an overcommitment problem, and perhaps worse, I had very loose focus in my research direction. This is something I'm in the process of fixing in my PhD. Stay tuned I guess. 

# Why statistics and academia?

I think for me, statistics was always what I wanted to do. I grew up with a statistician and academic for a father, so the field has always been a part of my life, from onset. I briefly flirted with other fields; in high school (and to this day--perhaps more casually), I loved history and English literature. If I didn't do STEM, I'd study one of those. For me, statistics is the most humanities-like of the CS/Math/Stats/Eng foursome. Interpretation, explaining statistics to laymen, interacting with the realities of what is feasible to model/experiment, and making a real-world impact... these all require substantial nonlinear thinking: the type that is characteristic of the liberal arts. And the nice thing with statistics is that you can always modulate how pie-in-the-sky you want to get (theory) and how down-and-dirty (applied) you want to be. I want to be somewhere in the middle. Methodology? Probably.

And academia? Two reasons. I love teaching. I love helping others. And being in academia allows me to be a teacher, and give back -- in much the same way I wish people did for me. Secondly, I love having the freedom to explore the research problems I care about. The flexibility of the job is also really appealing to me. I always wanted to be a professor, even as a child. And as I've grown up, my experiences have only hardened that desire. 

# Any advice?

Never, ever, wait for people to offer things for you. In life, the only person you can control is yourself. On the personal level, that includes being a good, decent, caring person. And on the work level, that always means working hard. No matter what. If you want something, work for it. Take (caluclated) risks. Take chances. And throw yourself into the deep end. 

Let me give an example. Many people in undergraduate asked me how I published so much, and how I managed to work with so many professors. It's because behind the scenes, I never waited for structured research awards like most people do. At every moment, I emailed profs, talked to them, networked, and yes, applied to awards. From most things, I got rejected. I've been embarrassed. You know, the usual. But you know, if you try enough, you'll get something. And my record, as young as it is, is proof of that. When people say school is hard, and it's hard to get opportunities... my response is always a question: how hard have you tried, and how *effectively* have you worked?

Also, never ever forget to take a break when you need one. You're a human. Human's aren't machines. So be human. 

Finally, smile! Crack a joke. Make others happy. Life is short. So have fun!

I don't say all of this because I think I know something about life. I don't really. I'm only, at the time of writing, 23. But I do think that maybe my experiences might resonate with some people. I certainly hope they do.
